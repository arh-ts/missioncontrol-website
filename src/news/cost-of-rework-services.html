Key points

  • Rework is work done twice: corrections after review, second requests to clients, returned files and time written off. Most services firms never see it as one number.
  • It hides because finance records it in pieces: salary, write-offs, slower billing.
  • Quality engineering solved this decades ago with first pass yield and cost of poor quality. Both translate directly to services workflows.
  • Measure rework per workflow, not per person, then price it: rework hours at loaded cost, plus write-offs, plus delay.
  • A priced rework rate turns "be more careful" into "fix the step that causes it".

Ask a services firm about its biggest costs and you will hear salaries, rent and technology. Ask what it costs to do work twice and you will get silence. Not because the number is small. Because nobody has added it up.

Rework in services looks like small, reasonable things. A reviewer sends a file back. A client is asked for a document they already sent. A partner writes off two hours because the junior took a wrong turn. A claim bounces between teams because a field was missing. Together they are, in our view, the largest cost line most services P&Ls never show.

The fix is not a culture campaign. It is measurement: treat rework as a flow through each workflow, count it and price it.

Why rework never shows up in the accounts

Manufacturing learned to see this cost long ago. ASQ defines the cost of poor quality as the costs of providing poor quality products or services, split into internal failure costs (defects found before the customer receives the work) and external failure costs (defects found after). The Juran Institute argues that poor quality can cost an organisation 20 to 30 per cent of total annual revenue. Treat that as an advocate's estimate, not a services benchmark. As MIT Sloan Management Review noted, the method was built for factories and is rarely used in services.

In a factory, scrap sits in a bin. In a services firm, rework disappears into three places:

  • Salary. Correction hours are paid whether or not they are billed. They look like ordinary capacity.
  • Write-offs and write-downs. Finance sees the number, but not the cause.
  • Delay. Every loop pushes out invoicing and cash. Nobody books that as a cost.

Thomas Redman called the hidden version of this the "hidden data factory": the extra steps people take to find, correct and verify data they do not trust. In that 2016 Harvard Business Review article he cites IBM's estimate that poor quality data cost the US $3.1 trillion a year, and estimates that knowledge workers lose as much as half their time to them. Both figures are rough and a decade old. The mechanism is unchanged.

What the evidence from professional services says

Law firms publish the most data on the financial end of rework. Every figure below carries a caveat about who collected it.

  • Clio's 2025 Legal Trends Report puts the average firm's realisation rate (billable work that gets invoiced) at 88 per cent and its collection rate at 93 per cent, from anonymised data on its own paying US customers. Clio sells practice management software, so its users are not a random sample.
  • Clio's mid-sized firm report, published as partner content on the Law Society site, found realisation of 83 per cent and collection of 84 per cent in 2023. In other words, about 17 per cent of billable hours never reached an invoice.
  • BigHand's sixth Law Firm Finance Report, published in January 2026 and based on more than 800 senior legal finance professionals, reported that nearly 90 per cent of firms saw increased write-offs, and 88 per cent expect further rises in 2026. BigHand is also a vendor.
  • The Thomson Reuters Law Firm Rates Report 2026, covering 195 US firms, found that firms grouped by heavy write-offs and write-downs still collected roughly what other groups did, between $553 and $580 an hour.

Not every write-off is rework. Some are discounts or scope disputes. But a meaningful share is time spent fixing or redoing work the client will not pay for twice. Realisation reports show the symptom, not the workflow that caused it.

Failure demand: rework that arrives from outside

John Seddon gave the other half of the picture a name. Failure demand is, in the words of a UK Government Digital Service post crediting him, "demand caused by a failure to do something or do something right for a customer". The client chasing an update. The call asking why an invoice is wrong.

The best evidence comes from the UK public sector. A study at Gloucestershire Constabulary, published in Public Money & Management, re-examined 534 non-urgent incidents and found about 30 per cent of non-urgent demand could be avoided. The same paper cites Seddon's estimate that failure demand accounts for 20 to 60 per cent of customer demand in financial services. In April 2026 the Strategy Unit, writing for the Health Foundation, published a review of failure demand in the NHS. It cites one GP practice where about a fifth of 599 consultations were attributed to system failures. It is candid that this is a single site and warns against making failure demand a performance target, because it invites gaming.

That caution applies to services firms too. Measure failure demand to find causes, not to rank people.

The measures: first pass yield and rework rate

Quality engineering offers two simple measures. First pass yield counts only items that came through a process as good with no rework. Rolled throughput yield multiplies the yield of each step to give the probability that a single item passes through the whole process without a defect.

That is why rework persists. Each team sees a respectable number for its own step. Nobody owns the product.

How to measure rework without monitoring individuals

The aim is to find the steps that generate rework, not the people who touch it. Rework is usually a property of the process: unclear instructions, missing inputs, an unwritten review standard. We cover the principles in how to map work without monitoring people. For rework specifically:

  1. Pick one workflow with a clear start, finish and review point: onboarding, monthly close, claims, conveyancing.
  2. Define what counts as a loop. A status moving backwards, a file reopened after review, a repeat client request, a credit note. Write the rules down first.
  3. Count loops from system events, not timesheets or screen activity. Practice management, ticketing and document systems already record status changes. Report at workflow and step level only.
  4. Classify each loop by cause: missing client input, internal error, changed instructions, unclear standard. A sample of 50 to 100 cases is enough.
  5. Estimate hours per loop from a short team workshop, not individual tracking. A range is fine.
  6. Join it to finance. Match write-offs and write-downs to the same workflows, and price the result with the formula above.
  7. Fix the top cause, then re-measure. Report first pass yield monthly, by workflow, alongside realisation.

The output is a short table: each workflow, its first pass yield, its annual cost of rework and its top two causes. It also feeds directly into an automation business case a CFO will accept, because the saving is measured, not assumed.

Mission Control maps how work moves between teams, including where it loops back, without reading content or ranking staff. But the method works with a spreadsheet. What matters is that someone finally adds the number up.

Questions

What is a rework rate in a services firm?

A rework rate is the share of work items in a workflow that need correcting, reopening or redoing before they are complete. It equals one minus first pass yield. Measure it per workflow, such as onboarding or monthly close, rather than per person.

How do you calculate the cost of rework?

Multiply rework hours by the loaded hourly cost of the people doing it, then add write-offs traced to rework and the cost of delayed invoicing. The hours are usually the largest part and the one most firms never count.

Is failure demand the same as rework?

They are close relatives. Rework is internal effort spent redoing work, while failure demand is extra demand from clients caused by something not being done, or not done right, the first time. Both are avoidable and both should be measured by workflow and cause, not used to rank staff.
