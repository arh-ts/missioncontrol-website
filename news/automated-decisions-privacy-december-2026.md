# Automated decisions: Australia's 10 December privacy deadline

> From 10 December, Australian privacy policies must describe where software makes or shapes significant decisions about people. You cannot disclose what you have not mapped.

Published 23 November 2026 by the Mission Control team. Canonical: https://missioncntrl.io/news/automated-decisions-privacy-december-2026

## Key points

- From 10 December 2026, Australian privacy policies must describe where computer programs make, or substantially and directly help make, decisions that could significantly affect people.

- The test is broad. Rule-based tools, spreadsheets and AI can all be caught, and human review of the output does not take a tool out of scope.

- The UK and US are moving the same way: new UK GDPR safeguards are in force, and California's rules apply from 1 January 2027.

- You cannot disclose automated decisions you have not mapped. Start with an inventory of where automation touches decisions about people.

- This is general information, not legal advice.

In less than three weeks, a new line appears in the Australian Privacy Principles. If your organisation uses a computer program to make, or to do something substantially and directly related to making, a decision that could significantly affect someone, your privacy policy has to say so.

Most organisations will treat this as a drafting job. It is not. The hard part is knowing what to write, and that depends on a question few operations teams can answer: where, exactly, does automation shape decisions about people? What follows is general information, not legal advice.

## What the Australian rule says

The [Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00128/asmade/2024-12-10/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html) received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. Part 15 of Schedule 1, on automated decisions and privacy policies, commences the day after 24 months from Royal Assent: 10 December 2026. It applies to decisions made after that date, even if the program and the data were in place earlier.

New APP 1.7 requires the privacy policy to contain extra information where three things are true:

- the entity has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making a decision;

- the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual; and

- personal information about the individual is used in the operation of the program.

APP 1.8 sets out what to disclose: the kinds of personal information used, the kinds of decisions made solely by the program, and the kinds of decisions where the program does a substantially and directly related thing. APP 1.9 adds that refusing or failing to make a decision counts, and that effects can be adverse or beneficial. Its examples include decisions affecting rights under a contract or access to a significant service or support.

The [OAIC has published](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making) a fact sheet, a flowchart and updated APP 1 Guidelines, shaped by 90 written submissions. [Allens reports](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/10/mandatory-automated-decision-making-disclosure-requirements-are-coming-key-takeaways-from-the-oaic-guidance/) the final guidance was released on 30 September 2026.

### Three points in the guidance that matter for operations

**"Computer program" is broad.** According to [Allens' reading of the guidance](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/10/mandatory-automated-decision-making-disclosure-requirements-are-coming-key-takeaways-from-the-oaic-guidance/), it covers rule-based tools, machine learning, generative AI and everyday software such as spreadsheets. An old eligibility formula in a workbook can count.

**Human review is not an exit.** The [OAIC fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF) says "substantially" means the output is a key factor in the human's decision, and "directly" means it has a direct connection with making it. Advisory outputs can qualify. Scope can apply even where the output is subject to human review. Machine learning or generative AI outputs used in significant decisions generally fall in scope unless subject to extensive human oversight and control.

**Significance has a low floor.** The fact sheet says the impact must be more than trivial. Its examples include recruitment software that sorts candidates, eligibility for insurance or housing assistance, approving or rejecting credit, and AI reports ranking employees for performance, promotion or bonuses. One example notes that using a program's rankings to decide whom _not_ to contact is still in scope.

The obligation sits with the user of the tool, [not the vendor](https://www.allens.com.au/insights-news/insights/2026/10/mandatory-automated-decision-making-disclosure-requirements-are-coming-key-takeaways-from-the-oaic-guidance/). [Gadens notes](https://www.gadens.com/insights/five-weeks-to-10-december-as-breaches-grow-and-frontier-ai-sharpens-cyber-threats,-transparency-on-adm-has-never-mattered-more) the OAIC's advice to take a cautious approach where uncertain.

## How the UK compares

The UK has loosened permission, not accountability. [Section 80 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80/enacted) replaces UK GDPR Article 22 with new Articles 22A to 22D. A decision is based solely on automated processing where there is "no meaningful human involvement". A decision is significant if it has a legal or similarly significant effect.

The [UK government describes](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/data-use-and-access-act-2025-data-protection-and-privacy-changes) the result as a more permissive framework: organisations can make these decisions in wider circumstances but must put safeguards in place. Under Article 22C, those safeguards must let people get information about the decision, make representations, obtain human intervention and contest the decision. Tighter limits remain where special category data is involved (Article 22B).

Section 80 came into force on 5 February 2026 under [Commencement Regulations No. 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/regulation/2/made). The ICO's [updated guidance on the safeguards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/automated-decision-making/what-are-the-adm-safeguards/) is still labelled draft. It is blunt on two points: human intervention "cannot be tokenistic", and spot checking an automated process does not meet the safeguard.

Recruitment is an early focus. In March 2026 the [ICO reported](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/03/automated-decisions-can-streamline-the-hiring-process-with-the-right-safeguards-in-place/) gaps in transparency, safeguards, consistency of human involvement and bias monitoring among employers using automated hiring.

## How the US compares

**California.** The [California Privacy Protection Agency announced](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250923.html) in September 2025 that its regulations were approved, effective 1 January 2026. Businesses using automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT) for significant decisions must comply from 1 January 2027. [Fisher Phillips summarises](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/new-california-regs-will-impact-your-ai-and-privacy-policies.html) ADMT as technology that processes personal information and uses computation to replace or substantially replace human decision making. Significant decisions cover financial or lending services, housing, education enrolment, employment or contracting, and healthcare. Businesses must give a pre-use notice, offer an opt-out or a human appeal, and describe access rights. [Hunton notes](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/cppa-finalizes-ccpa-regulations-on-automated-decision-making-technology-risk-assessments-and-cybersecurity-audits) that human reviewers must be able to evaluate the output and alter the final decision.

**Colorado.** The 2024 Colorado AI Act (SB24-205) was delayed from 1 February 2026 to 30 June 2026, then [repealed and replaced by SB 26-189](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/colorado-replaces-landmark-ai-act-an-overview-of-the-new-sb-26-189-framework.html), signed on 14 May 2026 and taking effect 1 January 2027. Deployers will need to give notice before using covered technology in a consequential decision, explain adverse decisions within 30 days, offer meaningful human review where commercially reasonable, and keep records for three years. The picture is still unsettled. A federal court in Colorado [paused enforcement](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/04/28/court-order-delays-enforcement-of-states-first-in-the-nation-ai-law/) in April 2026 pending a constitutional challenge, and [Eckert Seamans reports](https://www.eckertseamans.com/legal-updates/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-landmark-ai-statute-what-businesses-need-to-know) that Attorney General rulemaking had not formally started.

## Our view: you cannot disclose what you have not mapped

The three regimes share one dependency. Each assumes you know where software shapes a decision about a person, what data it uses, and what the human does with the output.

In most services organisations, nobody holds that picture. Automation arrives in pieces: a triage rule in the claims system, a score in the applicant tracking tool, a macro that flags overdue rent, an AI summary read before approving a credit limit. Together they form a decision system no one designed or documented.

So the privacy policy is the last step, not the first. Write it before you map the work and you will either under-disclose and carry the risk, or over-disclose in vague terms that help nobody. We have written before about [mapping how work moves without monitoring people](https://missioncntrl.io/news/map-work-without-monitoring-people). The same discipline applies here: trace the decision points, not the individuals.

## A practical readiness checklist

- **Inventory the decision points.** For each workflow touching clients, claimants, candidates, tenants or staff, list where decisions are made about a person, and every program feeding them: rules, scores, rankings, filters, summaries, flags. Include vendor tools and spreadsheets.

- **Classify significance.** Could the decision affect someone's rights or interests in a more than trivial way: a service, a contract, money, housing, employment, health? Record your reasoning for borderline cases.

- **Classify the program's role.** Does the program decide alone, or do something substantially and directly related? The policy must distinguish the two.

- **Check human involvement is real.** The OAIC fact sheet points to interrogating outputs, reviewing underlying data, using additional evidence and documenting reasons for departing from the output. If reviewers rarely override or cannot see the inputs, treat the human step as weak.

- **Read vendor contracts.** Confirm what data each tool uses. The disclosure obligation is yours, not the vendor's.

- **Update the privacy policy.** Cover the kinds of personal information and both kinds of decision. Group similar processes, but call out sensitive data clearly.

- **Keep records.** Give the inventory and review evidence an owner. In the UK, California or Colorado, the same records support safeguards, notices and appeals.

**Illustrative example**

A claims team uses a rules engine to pay low-value claims automatically, and a model score adjusters see when deciding whether to refer a claim for investigation. The automatic payments are decisions made solely by a program. The referrals involve a substantially and directly related program, because adjusters rarely depart from the score. Both belong in the privacy policy.

## Beyond 10 December

Treat the inventory as an operating asset, not a compliance file. Every new automation should pass through it before go-live. That keeps disclosures accurate and helps you decide [which automations are worth building](https://missioncntrl.io/news/why-automation-programs-stall) and which need a stronger human step. Mapping how work moves is a good starting point. The legal judgment on what to disclose stays with you and your advisers.

## Questions

### When does Australia's automated decision transparency obligation start?

It commences on 10 December 2026, two years after the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 received Royal Assent. It applies to decisions made after that date, even if the program and data were in place earlier.

### Does human review take a tool out of scope?

Not on its own. The OAIC says a program can be substantially and directly related to a decision even where its output is subject to human review. What matters is whether the output is a key factor and how genuinely the human tests it.

### What must the privacy policy say?

It must describe the kinds of personal information used by the programs, the kinds of decisions made solely by programs, and the kinds of decisions where programs do something substantially and directly related. Technical detail and commercially sensitive information are not required.
